> "Will AI 'steal' my job? Should I be worried? My daughter was interested in Computer Engineering, and now she is really looking at something else."

People are scared. When someone finds out I "lead a lot of AI efforts" at work nowadays, this is usually the first or second thing I get asked. I used to hedge and try to explain a bit, but now I've started just saying:

> Yes, you will lose your job to AI, and things will never be the same.
{: .pull-quote}

That breaks the ice so we can have the real conversation.

## I have "lost my job" at least 10 times

The truth is, I have "lost my job", by my count, at least 10 times now in my career. A few of these times were literal, when companies got bought or sold and layoffs happened. But most of the time new titles or roles or entire jobs opened up that never existed before, or the one I was in was simply needed less.

I started as a public school teacher, but soon became a "Computer Coordinator", then a "Director of Educational Technology" or a "Development Contractor" or "Web Architect" or "Innovation Architect" or "Software Consultant" or "Director of Solution Architecture".

Only the first one was on the list of jobs in the Career Center in school.

So let me ask you what I ask the people who ask me:

* Is what you are doing now what you went to school for?
* Did your job title even exist when you were in high school or college?

Many people will say no. And it is not just the people I happen to talk to. Stretch the clock out and it gets more dramatic: when MIT economist David Autor and his colleagues [went back through eight decades of job titles](https://news.mit.edu/2024/does-technology-help-or-hurt-employment-0401), they found that about 60 percent of the jobs people were doing in 2018 were types of work that did not exist in 1940.

AI is going to create a whole mess of new needs and amplify others, and yes, it will diminish the need for existing ones.

## The same job, every six years

Molly Graham recently recounted a conversation with journalist Manoush Zomorodi, who has spent 30 years in a profession that someone has been declaring dead the entire time. Journalism is still here. The day-to-day work of it just looks nothing like it used to. So Zomorodi reframed the question:

> "What would you do if you believed your job was always going to exist, it was just going to look completely different every six years?"
>
> <cite>Manoush Zomorodi, as told by Molly Graham in [Change Creates Opportunity. It Also Sucks.](https://mollyg.substack.com/p/give-away-your-legos-to-ai)</cite>

That is my whole point, framed better than I had managed. (By my math, mine has looked completely different more like every three or four years, but I will take six.)

## Who keeps moving the cheese?

If you have not read it, [*Who Moved My Cheese?*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Who_Moved_My_Cheese%3F) is Spencer Johnson's little 1998 parable about four characters in a maze whose cheese stops showing up where it always has. Some of them head out to find new cheese. One of them sits and waits for the old cheese to come back.

AI is "moving a lot of cheese." AI may even be "stealing your cheese." But the most important thing is to realize what this is:

> This is the natural order of things. It has always happened, and it will continue to happen. Plan for it - and even possibly get excited about it.
{: .pull-quote}

None of this is new. Back in 2007 I wrote about a t-shirt that read ["Made in America: By Robots"]({% post_url 2007-10-31-made-in-america-by-robots %}). Growing up near Detroit, with a grandpa who worked for Dodge, that one landed. The machine doing the replacing keeps changing. The worry does not - and I will not pretend the worry was wrong. "Natural" is not the same thing as "painless."

The part nobody warns you about is that every one of those new jobs meant being a beginner again. You go from being the person with the answers to being the person with the questions, and that is uncomfortable. But as I wrote [last time]({% post_url 2026-09-13-dont-get-cut-by-hanlons-razor %}), we are all novices somewhere - and right now, with AI, almost everyone is.

## About those "AI layoffs"

And there is the issue of all these mass layoffs being labeled as "AI". This post isn't about those, but let me say that I know several of the folks in the middle of many of these, and I am in the camp that sees little-to-no evidence of "masses of really smart and experienced people getting replaced by AI to do their existing jobs."

On paper, it looks like the opposite. AI is the reason employers gave for [more than 116,000 announced job cuts](https://www.challengergray.com/blog/challenger-report-august-job-cuts-up-58-consumer-products-food-lead/) through August of this year - more than any other reason. But that is the reason on the announcement, not a count of people a machine replaced. When Stanford's Digital Economy Lab [went through actual payroll records](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/) this summer, they did not find widespread job displacement from AI. They did not find experienced workers falling behind, either.

What they did find is a gap on the first rung of the ladder. Among 22-to-25-year-olds, employment in the occupations most exposed to AI is running about 19 percent behind employment in the less-exposed ones, and that gap has grown over the last year. The researchers are careful to say that no single study settles this - but it is not nothing.

## Back to the daughter

Which brings me back to the second half of that question, because that first rung is exactly what she is looking at.

I am not going to tell her that what she is seeing isn't real. But she is not standing on that rung today. She will get there in four or five years - most of a full turn of that six-year clock - and she is choosing based on a snapshot that will be out of date by the time she arrives.

And look at *what* is changing. The same researchers found that the work that is shrinking for young people is the "codified" kind - what can be taught from a textbook or written down as a procedure. The work that is growing, for people with experience, is the kind you only learn through practice, mentoring, and seeing a lot of real situations.

That is the difference between what I have called a [software laborer and a software craftsman]({% post_url 2023-09-13-Laborer-Craftsman-and-Artist %}). When everyone has a nail gun, it matters a lot more where the nail goes.

So I would not ask her "which job is safe?" Some are safer than others this year. None of them are safe for a whole career, and none of them ever were - the job she will have at forty is probably not on a Career Center list today either. I would ask her whether the problems in that field are ones she would want to keep working on, even if the job around them looks completely different every six years.

If the answer is yes, then go. Learn the fundamentals, get next to people who are really good at it, and expect the title to change. Mine did.

And that gives the rest of us a job to do too. Those first-rung jobs are where a lot of us learned the craft. If they are changing, then those of us who have been through this a few times need to make sure there is still a way in.

## So, yes

So, yes, I fully expect, if history serves, to lose a few more jobs along the way - and AI may be involved in there somewhere. But that's par for my experience, and it has helped to have gone through it with a lot of other great people who were also "looking for the next thing."

Be encouraged that this too will be a transition that we can make it through together.
